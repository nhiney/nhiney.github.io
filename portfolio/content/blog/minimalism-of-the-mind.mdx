One evening I opened Notion and realized I was trying to live seven life plans at once.

Learn a new framework. Write more consistently. Make my portfolio better. Read every day. Exercise. Study English seriously. Build a side project impressive enough to matter. Every item sounded reasonable. Every item sounded like something a young person with "direction" should do.

But looking at the whole list, I only wanted to close the laptop and sleep.

My mind at that moment felt like a room full of things. Nothing was exactly trash, which made it harder to remove. This shirt can still be worn. That book has not been read yet but maybe one day. This box might be useful later. Everything had a reason to stay, and because of that, the room had no space left to breathe.

My thinking was the same. I did not lack ideas. I was suffocating because too many ideas were demanding to live at the same time.

---

## Busyness can be a way to avoid choosing

I used to feel proud of having many plans. It made me feel as if I was moving forward, even when many things only sat still on a list. Whenever I worried about the future, I added a new goal. Another course, another habit, another three-month plan.

Planning can become addictive because it gives me the scent of progress before it asks me to pay with focus.

Choosing one thing to take seriously is much scarier. When I choose, I have to give up the image that I can become every version of myself at once. If I focus on writing, the side project slows down. If I put energy into work, I cannot learn three new things in the same month. If I want enough sleep, I have to stop performing as the person who always squeezes more in.

Mental minimalism does not begin with the question "What should I add to make my life better?" It begins with the more uncomfortable one: "What must I remove so the important thing has room to grow?"

That question touches the ego. I like believing I can handle everything. I want to be skilled, beautiful, healthy, deep, successful early, emotionally rich, and never missing out. I want a room full of belongings that still feels spacious.

It does not work that way.

My energy has an area. My time has an area. My attention has an even smaller area. I keep stuffing too many things into it not because I am greedy in an ugly way, but because I am afraid of choosing wrong. Afraid that if I let something go, I will regret it later. Afraid that someone else will progress exactly where I just stepped away.

But holding everything is also a choice. Usually, it is the choice that makes everything half-done.

---

## Clearing the mind hurts like clearing a room

When cleaning a room, the hardest part is not throwing away something I hate. That is easy. The hard part is holding something that once mattered, or still looks useful, and admitting: right now, it no longer serves my life.

Clearing the mind is the same.

I have to look at a few beautiful plans and say: not yet. Maybe later. Maybe never. I have to let go of imagined versions of myself so I can care for the real version that needs sleep, needs to finish the work, and needs less noise.

One month, I chose only two things: do my main work well and write more consistently. It sounded so small that my ego felt disappointed. Nothing grand to tell. Nothing that looked like "self-optimization."

But strangely, once I removed things, I began working more deeply. I stopped jumping from tab to tab to avoid difficulty. I stopped using busyness as proof that I was trying. A day had only a few things worth doing, so I had to actually face them.

One simple practice I use is writing three columns: things I am doing, things I am keeping out of fear, and things that can wait. The second column usually annoys me most. It contains good-sounding things: a course, a project, a habit, a promise to myself. But if I am honest, I know I am keeping them to feel safer, not to live better.

<KeyTakeaway label="Reminder to myself" tone="sage">
Focus is not iron discipline toward everything. Focus is being honest enough to admit that only a few things deserve my energy right now.
</KeyTakeaway>

Mental minimalism does not make my life poorer. It gives my life a path. A room with fewer things is not a room with less value. It is a room where what remains can be seen. A mind with fewer plans is not less ambitious. It is a mind that has stopped turning ambition into noise.

I still want to do many things. I do not want to become someone flat and uninterested. But I am learning to let desire pass through a narrower door. What matters this month? Which task, if finished, would make my life lighter? Which one only exists so I can look hardworking?

Some goals feel sad to put down. But sadness does not mean the choice is wrong. Many beautiful objects still have to leave a room if the room is meant to be lived in, not stored in.

That night, I deleted more than half the list. Not from my life forever. Just from the desk. I kept two things. Closed the laptop. Went to sleep.

The next morning, I did not wake up as a new person. But my head was less noisy. And sometimes, less noise is already a kind of luxury.
