The Fear of Being Ordinary

Why am I so afraid of becoming an ordinary person, and why can accepting ordinariness be a deep form of freedom?

Nguyen Thi Yen Nhi

June 27, 2026/5 min read/—views

One evening I was scrolling and saw three people my age living three very bright lives.

One received a scholarship. One stood on a stage speaking to more than a hundred people. One started a company, photographed at an angle beside coffee and a laptop full of stickers.

Meanwhile, I was lying on my bed, hair still damp, slightly hungry, wondering whether I should make instant noodles. The room was so quiet the fan sounded like it was mocking me.

A mean little question rose up: what if one day I am only an ordinary person?

Not a dramatic failure. Not a tragedy. Just ordinary. Go to work, come home, pay the electricity bill, do laundry on the weekend, sometimes happy, sometimes sad. No name in the news. No one speaking about me with admiration. No huge mark proving I was here.

That fear was not loud. It was like a small needle inside a shirt, rubbing against my skin whenever I saw someone else shining brighter.

I do not only want to live, I want to be confirmed

Since childhood, we are told many stories about special people. The main character always has something unusual. A talent, a destiny, a birthmark, a prophecy. Even when they begin very ordinary, the story eventually proves that they were not born to blend into the crowd.

Then we grow up, and social media feeds the same idea with real people. Anyone can become a "case study." Anyone can build a personal brand. Everyone should have a story compelling enough that others stop to listen.

Ordinary existence suddenly becomes a defective product, or at least a product that has not been marketed well.

I think the fear of being forgotten is not only ambition. It is deeper. It is the fear that if I do not stand out, I have no value. If I am not remembered, my life will pass like a feed item someone swiped away. If I have nothing to show, the days I lived might be considered wasted.

So I start turning everything into a test. Work has to have large meaning. Hobbies need growth potential. An article needs many readers. Even rest has to look tasteful.

Is that tiring? Very.

Because when life is forced to be memorable all the time, its most ordinary moments begin to be looked down on. A hot meal. An afternoon with no work messages. A day when I finish my tasks on time and no one praises me. Those things should already be warm enough, but I look at them with impatient eyes: is that all?

Being unseen is not the same as being unreal

It took me a long time to understand that the fear was not only about being ordinary.

I was afraid of being unconfirmed.

If nobody clapped, did the effort count? If nobody quoted the thing I wrote, was it still worth writing? If nobody could summarize my life into a clean impressive sentence, had I failed to become real enough?

That is a very tiring way to live, because it turns existence into a performance review. I start asking every day to justify itself. I ask rest to look intentional, work to look meaningful, kindness to be noticed, sadness to produce a lesson. Nothing is allowed to simply be.

Maybe ordinary life frightened me because so much of it cannot be displayed neatly. There is no dramatic caption for washing a bowl, taking a shower, answering one message honestly, or doing a task carefully without anyone seeing the before-and-after.

But private does not mean empty. A day can be lived fully even if it leaves no public artifact. A meal can feed me without becoming content. A kind sentence can matter even if nobody outside the room knows it happened. A piece of work can be careful even if it never becomes part of a story about my potential.

What I keep

A quiet life is not an erased life. Some value is real even when it is not witnessed, packaged, or turned into proof.

Accepting that I may be ordinary does not make me stop trying. If anything, it makes my effort cleaner.

I study because I want to understand, not only to impress. I write because there is something to say, not only to be remembered. I do good work because the work deserves care, not because it will turn me into a shining main character.

Freedom lives there. When I no longer require my life to look significant before it is worth living, I begin to receive the very small days I used to dismiss. Going to the market. Cooking a meal that does not burn. Finishing a task. Listening to a friend without holding my phone.

Those things do not make me immortal. But they make me present, and presence is not nothing.

If one day the wide world forgets me, maybe that is okay. What I hope for more is that people who passed through my life remember how it felt to be near me: a little easier to breathe, a little more respected, a little less alone.

A life like that may not be called great.

But it is not small.
