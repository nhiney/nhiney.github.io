Failure

The Romance of Private Failures

Not every failure gets a beautiful ending to tell. Sometimes I simply break something quietly, learn a little, and open the file again the next morning.

Nguyen Thi Yen Nhi

June 27, 2026/5 min read/—views

At two in the morning, the room held only the sound of the fan and the pale white light from my laptop.

I stared at a design file I had just deleted almost completely. No one knew I had sat with it for four hours. No one knew I had tried more than ten color versions, adjusted every bit of spacing, changed every line of text. And no one would know that in the end, it was still bad.

There was no post for that moment. No "behind the scenes" caption. No photo of a coffee cup beside the screen to prove I was hardworking. Only me, my aching back, and a very unpleasant feeling: I had truly tried, but had nothing to show.

People often tell failure stories after the failure has been redeemed by success. Failed an interview, then later joined a better company. Got rejected, then later won a big prize. Built a broken first project, then later became a founder.

In those stories, failure sounds poetic. It has a role. It is the quiet section before the music rises.

But there is another kind of failure that is not redeemed right away. No beautiful ending by the weekend. No sentence like "thank goodness that happened." It just lies there, a little ugly, a little embarrassing, not producing any achievement at all.

I want to talk about that kind.

Trying without an audience

I used to be afraid of invisible effort. Maybe because I live in a time when everything can become evidence.

Studying has grades. Work has KPIs. Exercise has before-and-after photos. Reading has stories. Even rest can become a set of photos with hot tea, candles, and a notebook by the window.

Effort that cannot be measured, posted, or retold feels unfair.

There are weeks when I learn something new and do not feel better at it. CV versions I revise again and again that still get no reply. Writing sessions where I produce two pages, then read them the next morning and wince. Conversations I prepare for carefully that still come out clumsy.

The childish part of me wants to be seen. Wants someone to know I tried. Wants a hand on my shoulder saying: yes, I saw it.

Without that, I start doubting the effort itself. If no one saw it, does it count?

Yes. But I had to grow up a little to believe that.

Because resilience is not only made in the moments when I stand up in front of people. It is also made in the moments when I stand up and no one knows I just fell. There is no applause, so the motive has to come from somewhere deeper. There is no quick reward, so persistence has to become less decorative. There is no witness, so I cannot perform for anyone.

Only one very honest question remains: if no one knows, will I keep going?

Quiet failure does not need makeup

I am not saying failure is beautiful in a way that romanticizes pain. Failure can be irritating. It makes me doubt my ability, snap at people who did nothing wrong, and want to close the laptop and live a life with no deadlines.

Do not force someone who has just been rejected to smile and say, "this is a lesson." Sometimes the lesson has not appeared yet. There is only anger.

But after a little time passes, private failures have a strange intimacy. They are where I meet myself without makeup.

No image of "a hardworking young person making progress." No resilient main character. No inspiring story to retell. Only a tired, embarrassed person deciding whether to open the file again tomorrow morning.

And that small decision is what matters.

Not a grand oath. Not a radiant transformation. Just opening the file again the next morning. Fixing one section. Sending one more email. Relearning something I thought I understood. Repeated enough times, those unglamorous actions quietly form something solid.

Pinned for myself

Not every effort needs to be seen to have value. Some of the strongest parts of me were trained where there was no spotlight.

I am beginning to feel more affection for the nights that produce no result. Not because I like failing, but because I know they are not completely useless.

They teach me to work when my ego does not get candy. They teach me to tell the difference between loving the work itself and only loving the image of myself doing the work. They teach me to stop needing an immediate beautiful ending before I believe I am still moving in the right direction.

If today you also tried at something no one knows about, and it still broke, I am not going to tell you to cheer up. Be angry. Lie still for a while. Let yourself feel a little pathetic if you need to.

But do not conclude too quickly that those hours disappeared.

They did not. They stay in the way you are calmer next time. In the hand that shakes less when receiving feedback. In the ability to begin again without someone pulling you up. In a version of you that others may only see after you have "made it."

They will not see the quiet nights holding you up.

That is okay.

Not everything precious needs a witness.
