Boundaries

When Your Kindness Gets Used

Keeping a good heart does not mean letting everyone use you like a free service. Boundaries do not make you less kind; they help your kindness last longer.

Nguyen Thi Yen Nhi

June 27, 2026/5 min read/—views

The message arrived at 11:43 at night: "Can you look at this for me real quick? I have to submit it tomorrow."

"Real quick" sat there, small and harmless. But I knew this "quick" would take at least two hours. I was tired. My eyes stung. My own deadline was not finished. But my hand still opened the file.

Because I was afraid refusing would sound cold.

Because that person had once praised me for being helpful.

Because somewhere in my head there was an invisible rule: good people help.

Two and a half hours later, I sent back the revised version. They reacted with a heart. The next morning they submitted it. They did not mention my name. They did not ask how much I slept. Nothing especially terrible happened, except that inside me there was a low, simmering irritation, as if I had lent someone my whole house and then stood outside waiting on the porch.

That was not the only time.

And I guess you may have had those times too.

Kindness is easily mistaken for availability

At work, especially in fast-moving environments, helping each other sounds beautiful. Whoever knows more supports someone new. Whoever is free helps someone stuck. Whoever works faster teaches someone slower. Teamwork, right?

But because it sounds beautiful, being used can wear very kind clothing.

It does not enter by saying, "Please sacrifice yourself for me." It enters through smaller sentences: "You're good at this," "I only have one quick question," "You can do it much faster than me," "Last time, promise." And if I am someone soft-hearted, those sentences touch the weakest place: the need to be seen as a good person.

I used to think the problem was only that other people were careless. Later I had to look at the harder part: sometimes I also take a little identity from being needed.

Being asked for help makes me feel valuable. Being praised as enthusiastic makes me feel lovable. Being called a "savior" makes me forget that I am exhausted too.

So I say yes even when my heart is saying no, then later blame them for not understanding my limits.

But other people cannot respect a boundary I have never drawn.

This sounds harsh, but it freed me a lot. If I keep smiling, accepting, saying "it's okay," while secretly writing emotional debt in my head, then I am also participating in the play. Kindness that leaves me swallowing resentment every time will eventually turn bitter.

Boundaries do not make kindness worse

I used to fear that setting boundaries would make me selfish. Especially when I was young, new at work, and entering communities full of capable people, I wanted to be seen as easy to collaborate with. I was afraid "no" would make people think I was cold, difficult, or lacking team spirit.

But a reluctant yes is not kindness. It is a loan with interest.

I give my time, but secretly expect the other person to be grateful enough, tactful enough, and return the right amount of recognition. When they do not, I feel hurt. So maybe that "yes" was not as clean as I thought.

A clear refusal can be much kinder.

"I cannot look at it tonight. Tomorrow after three, I can give feedback for twenty minutes."

"For this part, I can suggest the direction, but you should revise it yourself."

"This week I cannot take on extra work outside my part."

Those sentences do not close the door completely. They put the hinge in the right place.

Kindness is not letting anyone use me according to their needs. Kindness is helping when I can, in a way that does not betray myself. Sometimes that means staying ten extra minutes to explain something to a new teammate. Sometimes it means saying no so I can protect energy for my own work. Both can be kind, if they come from clarity instead of fear.

Reminder to myself

Kindness needs a spine. Without it, it is easily bent into obedience and then praised as sweetness.

I still want to be a good person. I just no longer want my kindness to operate like a free 24/7 service.

I want to help people without resenting them afterward. I want to say yes from a wide heart, not from a tight throat. I want to say no without attaching an entire essay of apology.

Some people will not like this version of me. Especially people who were used to the version that always nodded. They may say I have changed. They may say I am no longer as helpful. That is okay. Sometimes people call me selfish only because I am no longer convenient.

If your kindness has ever been used, I do not want you to become cold. Coldness is too expensive a price.

But become a little clearer. Before saying yes, ask: do I truly want to help, or am I afraid of being judged? Do I have enough energy, or am I signing an emotional debt note? Can I help with one part while still keeping my part?

Keep your kindness.

Just do not let it walk alone. Let it walk with self-respect. When those two stand beside each other, I am no longer foolish. I am someone who still chooses softness, but can no longer be bent out of shape so easily.
