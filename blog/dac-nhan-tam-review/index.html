How to Win Friends and Influence People

A personal note on how I want to read Dale Carnegie's book: learning communication so I can listen, recognize others, and influence with more kindness.

Nguyen Thi Yen Nhi

June 30, 2026/6 min read/—views

There is one way of reading How to Win Friends and Influence People that makes me uneasy: treating it like a manual for making people like me more.

I think that way of reading makes the book smaller. If I only look for techniques that make me sound smoother, I can easily forget the more important question: am I using that tact to understand people, or to steer the conversation toward what I want?

This is the note I want to keep after reading it: learn communication so I can respect people better, not so I can become better at "winning" them over.

Good communication starts by moving myself out of the center

One idea that kept returning in my notes is this: the person across from me does not only need to hear me say the correct thing. They need to feel that I actually see them.

That sounds simple, but in everyday life it is not easy. When I argue, I often want to prove that my point makes sense. When I give feedback, I can stare too hard at the mistake that needs fixing. When I ask someone for help, I often begin from my own need.

One small way to shift that is to ask before speaking:

  • What does the other person care about right now?
  • Are they feeling criticized or diminished?
  • Can I say the problem clearly while still protecting their dignity?

Put simply, good communication is not about making myself more impressive in a conversation. It is about creating enough room that both people do not have to stay defensive.

Good communication is not about making myself stand out more in the conversation. It is about making the other person feel a little less alone while being heard.

Praise only has weight when it is specific and sincere

I like this idea because it is so ordinary. A vague compliment can sound pleasant, but it does not always land. A specific compliment shows that I was really paying attention.

Instead of saying, "You are so good at this," I can say, "I like how you explained this part because it made a difficult idea easier to understand." The second sentence is smaller, but it is more real.

It also reminds me that kindness should not become a step in a process for creating affection. If I praise someone only so they will agree with me more easily, they will usually sense it. But if I recognize something because I truly saw their effort, that small sentence can make someone else's day much lighter.

Kind recognition does not make another person dependent on my praise. It helps them see their own effort more clearly.

Disagreement does not have to make someone feel smaller

There are times when I need to give feedback, say "this part is not working," or defend my point of view. But the way I say it changes a lot.

"You are wrong" may be accurate as information, but it often closes the conversation. A softer way is: "I may be understanding this differently, but I want us to look at this point together." The goal is still to fix the issue, but the feeling the listener receives is completely different.

I do not think kind communication means avoiding the truth. In fact, it asks me to speak more clearly, but without turning the truth into a blow against someone else's ego.

Truth is easier to receive when it does not arrive with the feeling of being insulted.

Sincerity is the hardest part

The easiest thing to do with a book about communication is turn everything into a formula. When meeting someone, do this. When trying to create goodwill, say that. When a conversation gets tense, choose this other move.

Formulas can help, but without sincerity, people notice very quickly. They can usually feel the difference between genuine attention and someone merely completing the right step.

I like to think of sincerity as what keeps skill ethical. I can learn to speak more gently, but not to hide what I really think. I can learn to care about other people's feelings, but not to control them. I can want to influence, while still respecting another person's right not to agree.

That is the hardest part: using tact to make relationships clearer and kinder, not to make everything bend toward me.

Tact in communication should not be a coat of paint over a selfish purpose.

Influence needs boundaries

The thing I want to hold most carefully while reading a book about influence is boundaries. Influence can be beautiful when it helps someone see a better possibility, dare to try, or cooperate more freely. But influence can also slide into manipulation if the only goal is making people follow what I want.

So before applying any communication "tip," I want to ask myself:

  • Am I trying to understand the other person better, or only trying to make them react the way I want?
  • Am I truly listening, or only waiting for my turn to persuade?
  • Am I leaving the other person the right to disagree?

For me, that boundary matters a lot. Kindness is not making everyone pleased with me. Tact is not speaking so everyone always agrees. Sometimes mature communication means being both gentle and very clear about what I should not trade away.

What I want to keep

Reading How to Win Friends and Influence People is most useful to me when I treat communication as a way to respect people, not as a set of techniques for winning conversations.

One small thing to try now

If I want to apply this right away, I think I can start very small: in the next conversation, try listening one sentence longer before responding.

Instead of answering immediately, ask: "Do you mean...?", "How did that feel?", or "What do you need most right now?" These small questions do not erase my own point of view, but they help the other person feel they are not being skimmed over too quickly.

I may not become a wonderfully skillful communicator after one book. But if each day I criticize a little less, recognize effort a little more specifically, and listen a little more deeply, the conversations around me may become a little kinder too.
