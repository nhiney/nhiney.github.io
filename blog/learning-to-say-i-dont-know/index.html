Learning to Say 'I Don't Know' in a World That Loves Sounding Smart

Sometimes the most mature answer is not sounding impressive, but naming a gap and learning it honestly.

Nguyen Thi Yen Nhi

June 27, 2026/6 min read/—views

The Google Meet screen glowed blue. A question had just landed, lightly, but enough to make my heart drop for a second.

Someone asked about a technical concept I had heard before, skimmed before, and confidently filed away as "I probably know this roughly." But when I had to say it out loud, my mind became a very clean blank space.

I smiled. The polite kind of smile that buys three more seconds of thinking.

Those three seconds felt unbearably long.

Inside my head, there were two voices. One said: just say something, use a few terms that sound right, maybe no one will check. The other was smaller, but more uncomfortable: stop performing, you really do not know.

In the end I said, "I do not have a firm grasp on this part yet. Let me read again and answer more carefully."

The room did not explode.

No one stood up and pointed at me. No chair was pulled out to remove me from the technology field. There was only a very normal nod, and then the meeting continued.

The strange thing was that afterward, I felt lighter. Not light like winning. Light like putting down a backpack I had worn for too long: the backpack of always needing to look like I know.

The hardworking pride of someone still learning

At twenty-one, it is very easy for me to feel as if everyone is moving faster.

LinkedIn is full of certificates. Facebook is full of achievements. People talk about AI, cloud, product, data, and startups with such smoothness that it sounds as if they were born understanding those things. If I fall behind by even one beat, I immediately feel like someone lost in the wrong classroom.

Because I am afraid of being found out, I learned a dangerous skill: pretending to understand.

Nodding when I hear a new term. Smiling when someone jokes about a technology I have never used. Saving an article and never reading it, but feeling calmer because I "know it exists." Sometimes I am not really learning. I am collecting props so I can look like someone who is learning.

I do not think this is only a personal flaw.

Modern environments reward surface quickness. Whoever answers quickly sounds competent. Whoever uses the right keywords sounds up to date. Whoever says "I do not know yet" is afraid of being seen as weak, slow, or not serious enough.

So I learned to speak neatly, smoothly, and just enough to get through.

But knowledge does not care how well I perform. Not knowing is still not knowing. No matter how many layers of terminology I put over that gap, the hole remains.

"I don't know" is not the end

I used to think saying "I do not know" was the same as announcing that I was not good enough.

Later I saw that it is more like a doorway.

As long as I pretend to know, I cannot really learn. To learn, I first need a blank space that is named correctly. Someone who is busy decorating emptiness into intelligence has no room left for questions.

Once, I was reading documentation for a new framework. The first three paragraphs used words I recognized, but the sentences still felt strange. The old me would have scolded myself: why can other people understand this and I cannot? Then I would have skimmed quickly, marked it as "read" in my head, and continued living with a hollow kind of knowledge.

That day I did something else.

I opened a note and wrote the first line: "I do not understand this lifecycle."

It felt embarrassing to write. But that embarrassment saved me. From one very specific "I do not know," I found a specific question. From a specific question, I found a specific example. By the end of the session, I still was not good at it. But at least I was no longer vaguely sophisticated about something I did not understand.

Some "I don't know" sentences make the ego smaller, but they let real ability grow.

Being honest about a knowledge gap does not make me less intelligent. It only takes away my right to live in illusion. And sometimes the most painful part is not that I do not know, but that I no longer get to pretend I do.

What I want to keep

Courage is not always saying something sharp. Sometimes courage is letting a gap become visible, then patiently filling it with real learning.

A truthful answer can still be professional

Saying "I do not know" does not mean giving up or pushing responsibility away.

It becomes much more trustworthy when it comes with a clear next step.

"I do not have a firm grasp on this part yet. I will reread the documentation and send a more careful answer before this afternoon."

"I have not used that tool in a real project, but I understand the basic concept. I need to check more before I conclude."

"I do not want to answer carelessly. Give me a little time to confirm."

Those sentences do not make me smaller. They show other people that I know the boundary of my understanding, and that I respect accuracy more than pride.

There are still so many things I do not know. Some days that scares me. Some days it makes me laugh. But the further I go, the more I believe that the admirable person is not the person who is never stuck.

The admirable person is the one who does not turn being stuck into a performance.

I want to become that kind of person: able to say "I do not know" without feeling that I have collapsed; not using confidence to cover lazy learning; calm enough to begin from the smallest question.

Because real learning often starts in an ugly place. It starts with a warm face, a touched ego, and the feeling that I have become small.

But if I am willing to stand in that small place long enough, I may find something kinder:

Not knowing is not a crime.

Refusing to know that I do not know is the trap.
